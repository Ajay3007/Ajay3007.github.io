H-Index — LeetCode 274 — the intuition that makes it click 👇

The definition trips everyone up: your h-index is h if you have h papers each cited at least h times (and it's the biggest such h).

Here's the intuition. Sort your papers by citations, most-cited first. Now at rank i, you already have i papers that are at least this cited. So if the paper at rank i STILL has ≥ i citations, you've got i papers with ≥ i citations → h is at least i. Keep going until it breaks.

Picture the sorted bars and a "citations = rank" line: your h-index is exactly where the bars cross that line. 📉📈

For [3,0,6,1,5] → sorted [6,5,3,1,0]:
rank 1: 6≥1 ✓ · rank 2: 5≥2 ✓ · rank 3: 3≥3 ✓ · rank 4: 1≥4 ✗
→ h = 3 (three papers with ≥3 citations) ✅

→ O(n log n) with sorting (O(n) with counting sort)

H-Index — LeetCode 274 (The Intuition, Visualized)

-- Description --
H-Index (LeetCode 274), explained with the intuition that makes it click.

The definition is the confusing part: your h-index is h if you have h papers each cited at least h times, and it's the largest such h. The clean way to see it: sort your papers by citations, most-cited first. At rank i you already have i papers that are at least this cited, so if the paper at rank i still has at least i citations, then you have i papers with at least i citations — meaning the h-index is at least i. Walk down until that stops being true, and the last rank where it held is your answer.

Visually, plot the sorted citations as descending bars and draw the line "citations = rank." The h-index is exactly where the bars cross that line — the largest h x h square that fits under the bars.

Example: [3,0,6,1,5] -> sorted [6,5,3,1,0]. Rank 1: 6>=1, rank 2: 5>=2, rank 3: 3>=3, rank 4: 1>=4 fails -> h = 3.

⏱️ O(n log n) time with sorting, O(1) space. You can also do it in O(n) time with counting sort: bucket the papers by citation count, then scan from the top accumulating counts.
